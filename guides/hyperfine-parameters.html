Hyperfine parameters

Hyperfine parameters are the values found by fitting a Mössbauer spectrum. Each comes from a different interaction between the iron nucleus and its surroundings. Move the sliders to see what each one does.

Isomer shift (δ)

The isomer shift comes from the interaction between the nucleus and the electrons around it. It moves the whole spectrum left or right. Strictly, it is only called the isomer shift when the source and the sample are at the same temperature. Otherwise centre shift is the better term, although the two are often used interchangeably.

The isomer shift is given relative to a calibration, usually metallic iron (α-Fe) at room temperature, which is defined as 0 mm/s. It is very sensitive to the oxidation state. At room temperature, high-spin Fe(III) usually lies between about 0.2 and 0.5 mm/s and high-spin Fe(II) between about 0.9 and 1.3 mm/s.

Quadrupole splitting (ΔEQ)

The excited state of the iron-57 nucleus is not spherical. When the electric field around it is not symmetrical, because of the iron's own electrons or the atoms around it, the single line splits into two. This pair is called a doublet. The isomer shift is the midpoint of the two lines and the quadrupole splitting is the distance between them.

The quadrupole splitting also depends on the oxidation state. High-spin Fe(II) usually has a large splitting (often above 1.5 mm/s), while high-spin Fe(III) usually has a smaller one (often below 1 mm/s). Plotting δ against ΔEQ is a quick way to tell the two apart.

Hyperfine field (Bhf)

When the iron atoms in a mineral are magnetically ordered, the nucleus feels a strong magnetic field. This splits the spectrum into six lines, called a sextet. The larger the field, the further apart the lines. In a powder, where the crystals point in all directions, the six lines have areas in the ratio 3:2:1:1:2:3.

The total area stays the same, so as the lines spread out each one becomes shallower. Metallic iron has a field of about 33 T and hematite about 52 T, which is why spectrometers are usually run from −12 to +12 mm/s. A narrower range would cut off the outer lines.

Quadrupole shift (ε)

In a sextet, the quadrupole interaction no longer splits lines into pairs. Instead it shifts the two outer lines one way and the four inner lines the other, so the sextet becomes slightly lopsided. This is the quadrupole shift. It is related to the quadrupole splitting by ε = (ΔEQ/2)(3cos²θ − 1)/2, where θ is the angle between the magnetic field and the main axis of the electric field.

The quadrupole shift is usually small compared with the hyperfine field, but it can help identify minerals. Goethite, for example, has a clearly negative ε. Take care when reading papers, because some report 2ε instead of ε, and some call it quadrupole splitting.

Linewidth

Each line has a width, usually given as the full width at half maximum (FWHM). The natural linewidth of iron-57 is 0.097 mm/s, and because both the source and the sample contribute, the narrowest line you can measure is about 0.19 mm/s. Thick samples, the instrument and a spread of slightly different iron environments all make lines broader.

Relative area

When a spectrum is fitted with several sites, the share of the total area in each site gives an estimate of how much of the iron is in that environment, for example the Fe(II) to Fe(III) ratio. Different environments can absorb with different efficiency (the recoil-free fraction), so the estimate is approximate, especially at room temperature.
